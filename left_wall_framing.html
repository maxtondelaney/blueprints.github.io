    This wall is a bearing wall. Unlike the front and rear gable walls, the left wall carries the rafters: each rafter bears on its double top plate with a 3-1/2" birdsmouth seat, and the tributary load is half the 12'-0" roof span, so roughly 6'-0" of roof depth per foot of wall. Four rafters land within the window opening, so the header carries real roof load rather than just wall weight.
    Assumed load path (REQUIRES STRUCTURAL REVIEW — NOT ENGINEER APPROVED): 2×8 rafters → birdsmouth seat on double 2×6 top plate → studs / 6×8 window header → jack studs → continuous pressure-treated bottom plate → slab. There is no post anywhere in this wall: the header bears on the jack studs, and the three-stud framed corners at each end land on that same plate rather than on the slab. Uplift is resisted by a hurricane / rafter tie at every rafter, and at the base by hold-downs at the framed corners.

    Base of wall: the framed corners bear on a continuous pressure-treated bottom plate rather than landing on the slab in standoff bases. Every bottom plate in this building is pressure treated, over a capillary break. Both arrangements are conventional. This one was chosen because the slab is already poured: a continuous plate lets the anchors be placed inboard and spread out instead of forcing one into the corner of the slab where edge distance and breakout are worst, it lets the plate be set and drilled through in place, and it gives the sheathing a continuous member to deliver shear into. Bearing is not the deciding factor at these loads — a three-stud corner puts about 20-1/4 sq in of wood on the plate rather than the 30-1/4 sq in a 6×6 would, and the load at the end of this wall is a fraction of the concentrated ridge reaction the front wall carries, so crushing the plate is not the governing concern here. The trade is that the plate must be pressure treated over a capillary break, hardware in contact with it must be corrosion resistant, and a member toe-nailed to a plate has no uplift value — so hold-downs, shown as symbols only, do that work at the corners. Those are the same pier-end hold-downs called for in the shear / bracing note above: each of the two sheathed piers ends at a framed corner, so the corner symbols and the bracing note's pier-end hold-downs describe one requirement, not two. The portal frame tie-downs at the two king studs beside the window are a separate requirement, at the opening-side edge of each panel rather than at the pier end, and they are additional to the corner hold-downs, not an alternative to them — four base tie-down symbols in all. All of them, and the plate anchors, are post-installed because the slab is already poured. The base detail and the 30" stone veneer detail have to be resolved together, since the veneer needs a weep screed and drainage gap and the bottom of the framing needs clearance above finished grade. Load estimate is approximate and for context only; anchor type, spacing, edge distance, embedment, hold-down selection and concrete capacity are all per engineer.
    Corners: both ends of this wall are standard three-stud 2×6 framed corners rather than solid 6×6 posts, which leaves no post anywhere in this wall — the 6×8 window header bears on the jack studs, not on posts, so there is no beam-to-post bracket on this sheet. The corner block still takes up 5-1/2" of wall length, because that is the thickness of the wall returning at right angles, so nothing in the horizontal layout moved: the wall still totals 120", the stud infill between the corners is still 109", the infill bays are unchanged, and the sheathed pier widths and aspect ratios are unchanged because a pier is measured from the end of the wall to the edge of the opening and always contained the corner. Inside the block sit a 2×6 on edge at each end and a 2×6 laid flat at the interior face between them, backing the drywall return on the end wall, with the void behind the flat member left for insulation. One consequence is worth naming, and it lands directly on the portal frame detail: the pier chord at each corner is now three separate 2×6 instead of one solid post, so those members have to be nailed or bolted together to act as one before a hold-down can develop tension in them. A single 2×6 will not. The portal frame's outboard chord and pier-end tie-down location at each end of this wall is one of these framed corners, so the ganging is part of what makes the portal frame work rather than a separate carpentry detail. Ganging and hold-down attachment are per engineer.
    Sheathing: the building gets a full plywood wrap — every wall sheathed in 21/32" (5/8" nominal) plywood, and the roof deck the same panel. On this sheet the wall sheathing is noted rather than drawn, because in an elevation the panel faces the viewer and has no visible thickness; only the roof deck carries its 21/32" into a dimension, in the top-of-sheathing height at the ridge. Worth being clear about what this does and does not buy: 21/32" is thicker than the 7/16" or 15/32" normally run for shear, so panel shear capacity is not what constrains this wall. The constraints are the pier lengths and aspect ratios described below, and a thicker panel does not relax either rule — which matters here, because these piers are over the 3.5:1 limit and that is the whole reason the portal frame detailing is on the sheet. Span rating, edge nailing, blocking and the bracing schedule are per engineer.
