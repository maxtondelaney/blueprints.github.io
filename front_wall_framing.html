    Base of wall: the 6×6 door posts and the framed corners bear on a continuous pressure-treated sill plate rather than landing on the slab in standoff post bases. Every bottom plate in this building is pressure treated, over a capillary break. Both arrangements are conventional. This one was chosen because the slab is already poured: a continuous plate lets the anchors be placed inboard and spread out instead of forcing one into the corner of the slab where edge distance and breakout are worst, it lets the plate be set and drilled through in place, and it gives the sheathing a continuous member to deliver shear into. Bearing is not the deciding factor at these loads — the most heavily loaded post carries on the order of 1,500 lb over 30-1/4 sq in, roughly 50 psi against a perpendicular-to-grain allowable several times that, so crushing the plate is not the governing concern. The trade is that the plate must be pressure treated over a capillary break, hardware in contact with it must be corrosion resistant, and a post toe-nailed to a plate has no uplift value — so the two 6×6 door posts get a post-to-base connector at the foot where they land on the plate, and hold-downs, shown as symbols only, do the pier-chord work above that. The two are not the same thing: the base connector locates the post and takes uplift and shear at the post itself, while the hold-down resists overturning of the sheathed pier as a whole. The base detail and the 30" stone veneer detail have to be resolved together, since the veneer needs a weep screed and drainage gap and the bottom of the framing needs clearance above finished grade. Load estimate is approximate and for context only; anchor type, spacing, edge distance, embedment, hold-down selection and concrete capacity are all per engineer.
    Corners: the four corners are standard three-stud 2×6 framed corners rather than solid 6×6 posts. The corner block still takes up 5-1/2" of wall length, because that is the thickness of the wall returning at right angles, so nothing in the horizontal layout moved: the wall still totals 144", the infill bays are unchanged, and the sheathed pier widths and aspect ratios are unchanged because a pier is measured from the end of the wall to the edge of the opening and always contained the corner. Inside the block sit a 2×6 on edge at each end and a 2×6 laid flat at the interior face between them, backing the drywall return on the side wall, with the void behind the flat member left for insulation. One consequence is worth naming: the pier chord at each corner is now three separate 2×6 instead of one solid post, so those members have to be nailed or bolted together to act as one before a hold-down can develop tension in them. A single 2×6 will not. Ganging and hold-down attachment are per engineer.
    Brackets: a beam-to-post bracket is shown wherever a beam or header lands on a post — the 6×8 door header onto each 6×6 door post, and the 6×10 ridge beam onto the 6×6 ridge post. Each is drawn sized off the members it joins, with a 5-1/2" seat matching the member width and straps running up the beam and down the post, but the symbol is not a product: model, capacity, bearing area and fastener schedule are per engineer. An H-style bracket ties the bottom of the ridge post down to the double top plate, with a strap on each post face and a crossbar at the plate line. There is one per ridge post, so two in the building. That joint is also where the ridge reaction crosses the plates in compression perpendicular to grain, and the bracket restrains the post without relieving that bearing check.
    Birdsmouth seat: rafters bear on a 3-1/2" horizontal seat cut with a plumb heel cut at the outer wall face, leaving 6-3/8" height above plate. The notch is 1-9/16" measured perpendicular to the rafter, about 22% of the 7-1/4" depth, which stays inside the quarter-depth end-notch limit usually applied to sawn lumber — confirm against the current CRC and with the engineer, along with required bearing length and perpendicular-to-grain bearing. Cutting the seat lowers the whole roof plane 1-3/4"; the pitch stays 6:12. Common rafters bear this way on the left and right wall plates; the rake rafter is notched the same at both corners of this wall.
    Sheathing: the building gets a full plywood wrap — every wall sheathed in 21/32" (5/8" nominal) plywood, and the roof deck the same panel. The wall sheathing is noted rather than drawn, because in an elevation the panel faces the viewer and has no visible thickness; only the roof deck shows its 21/32" in section at the rake. Worth being clear about what this does and does not buy: 21/32" is thicker than the 7/16" or 15/32" normally run for shear, so panel shear capacity is not what constrains this wall. The constraints are the pier lengths and aspect ratios described above, and a thicker panel does not relax either rule. Span rating, edge nailing, blocking and the bracing schedule are per engineer.
    Connections: the ridge connection is a sloped rafter hanger at every rafter, into the side faces of the structural ridge beam; uplift is a hurricane / rafter tie at every rafter-to-wall connection, over the birdsmouth seat and onto the double top plate. Both are drawn as hidden-line symbols only — the rafters they serve are beyond this wall plane, and model, capacity, slope/skew and fastener schedules are not selected.

    Rake overhang: 12" at both gables, framed off the ridge beam rather than off the gable wall. The 6×10 runs 12'-0" overall — 10'-0" between the gable walls plus a 12" cantilever at each end — and carries a 2×8 fly rafter at the outer edge, with 2×6 lookouts tied back to the first common rafter. The whole assembly projects out of the plane of this view, so the fly rafter reads coincident with the rake rafter drawn here and the lookouts fall hidden behind it. Two things are still open: lookout spacing along the rake, and how the lookouts pass the gable-end rafter — dropping that rafter flush with the lookout tops would shorten the gable studs shown on this sheet. The beam cantilever and the fly-rafter reaction at its tip need to be checked with the beam.
